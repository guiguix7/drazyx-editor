# Music Energy, Mood & Visual Art Direction

This reference is mandatory for substantial music-video, Reel, TikTok, Short, visualizer, and music-promo edits. Read it before choosing pacing, motion, transitions, color, texture, typography, or the opening hook.

Its job is to turn a listener's description—"upbeat", "melancholic", "danceable", "sad", "motivational", "vibe", "edit"—into specific editorial decisions. Do not stop at mood adjectives.

## 1. Diagnose the music before designing the edit

Listen to the full track when available, then listen closely to each candidate excerpt. Build a section-level profile: a track can change energy, emotion, groove, density, and tension between intro, verse, drop, breakdown, bridge, and ending.

Do not infer mood from genre, BPM, key, waveform height, or one instrument alone. A slow track can feel powerful; a fast track can feel sad; a minor-key track can be playful; a melancholic melody can sit over a danceable groove. Audio analysis is supporting evidence, not a substitute for listening.

Record these dimensions using relative editorial ratings. They are planning aids, not scientific measurements:

| Dimension | Rating | What to listen for | What it controls visually |
|---|---:|---|---|
| Perceived energy | 1–5 | Overall activation, urgency, impact, and intensity | Motion intensity, edit density, scale of accents |
| Groove / dance drive | 1–5 | How strongly the rhythm invites body movement; pulse, swing, syncopation | Accent placement, movement rhythm, cut relationship |
| Emotional valence | -2 to +2 | Dark/negative ↔ bright/positive emotional color | Light, warmth, contrast, visual openness |
| Tension | 1–5 | Suspense, instability, pressure, anticipation, unresolved harmony | Camera stability, contrast, buildup, release |
| Musical density | 1–5 | Number and activity of audible layers/events | Visual complexity, number of simultaneous elements |
| Spatial openness | 1–5 | Intimacy/claustrophobia ↔ spaciousness/air | Negative space, framing, blur, depth, movement scale |

Also state:
- Primary mood and secondary mood, using precise words rather than only a genre.
- Emotional contradiction, if present (for example, melancholic but danceable).
- Trajectory: rising, falling, steady, suspended, drop-and-release, alternating, or another evidence-based description.
- Signature event: the melody, bass movement, drum entrance, pause, fill, chord change, texture, vocal, or effect tail that gives the excerpt its identity.
- Confidence: high / medium / low, with uncertainty stated when the audio was not fully accessible.

For every selected excerpt, write a timestamped event map with the actual audio event and the likely visual opportunity. Example: "00:08.4 — kick and bass enter; first rhythmic camera accent", not "00:08 — big beat" unless that is what is heard.

### Mood is not the same as energy or groove

Keep these concepts separate:
- Energy describes activation/intensity.
- Groove describes rhythmic movement and dance drive.
- Mood describes emotional character.
- Valence describes positive/negative emotional color.
- Tension describes pressure and expectation.
- Density describes how much is happening sonically.
- Atmosphere describes the perceived space, texture, and world of the track.

Never flatten a mixed track into one label. A wistful, sad melody over club drums needs a different visual grammar from a slow, beatless sad piano track.

## 2. Translate the profile into a visual grammar

Use this relationship as the starting rule:

- Energy controls how active the edit may become.
- Groove controls when movement accents land.
- Mood and valence control the emotional character of framing, light, color, and typography.
- Tension controls how the edit builds, holds, destabilizes, and releases.
- Density controls visual complexity.
- Trajectory controls the arc from opening to payoff.

Choose a visual grammar before editing. Define cut cadence, camera behavior, motion range, transition behavior, lighting, texture, typography, and why each is appropriate.

### Energy scale: starting ranges, not fixed presets

| Perceived energy | Editing pace | Motion behavior | Accent behavior |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 — very low / suspended | Long phrase-led holds; often 4–8 seconds or longer when music sustains them | Slow drift, nearly imperceptible push/pull, stable framing | Rare; use phrase changes, sustained notes, or reverb tails rather than kicks that are not present |
| 2 — low / restrained | Deliberate visual changes, often around 3–6 seconds | Smooth reframing, restrained parallax, soft lighting evolution | A few accents per phrase at most; keep scale and rotation changes small |
| 3 — moderate / flowing | Clear visual development, often around 2–4 seconds | Mixed holds and movement; alternate push, pull, and lateral motion | Select important beats, fills, bass accents, or melodic turns |
| 4 — high / driving | More frequent subdivisions, often around 1–3 seconds when arrangement supports them | Confident reframing, short camera punches, faster but controlled transitions | Use selected bar, kick, snare, bass, or fill accents; leave recovery space |
| 5 — very high / explosive | Fastest pace in the project, still organized by phrases and musical hierarchy | Decisive moves, brief energetic changes, strong contrast between impact and stillness | Accentuate only the strongest events; build rests and quieter contrast into the edit |

These intervals are editorial starting points, not a rule to insert a cut every few seconds. A held frame is valid when it sustains a meaningful musical or emotional moment. If the frame, music, and information all remain unchanged without intentional tension, revise the passage.

### Mood-to-editing matrix

| Musical character | Camera / framing | Cut and transition language | Light, color, and texture | Avoid |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Melancholic / wistful | Slow push or drift, restrained rotation, thoughtful negative space, occasional tighter crop on a melodic detail | Longer holds; changes on phrase turns, harmonic shifts, or emotional accents | Muted or cool tonal balance, soft highlights, subtle grain, restrained bloom | Constant shake, rapid cuts, hard flashes, exaggerated bounce |
| Sad / intimate / vulnerable | Close, steady, personal framing; small movements; let empty space carry emotion | Minimal cuts; protect vocal/melodic phrases and quiet moments | Soft contrast, gentle falloff, delicate texture, low-intensity light | Fake dramatic breakdowns, melodramatic typography, forced hopeful rise |
| Melancholic + danceable | Keep an immersive, composed frame but let selected bass/kick moments move the image | Phrase-led holds combined with syncopated accents; contrast stillness and groove | Wistful palette with precise, restrained highlights; retain atmosphere | Treating it as either a static sad edit or a generic club visualizer |
| Dreamy / atmospheric / "vibe" | Slow layered drift, controlled parallax, gradual crop changes, selective focus | Long transitions, dissolves, phrase-led reveals; avoid busy cutting | Diffused light, depth, soft bloom, fine grain, space | Assuming "vibe" means dark purple, constant blur, or no movement |
| Upbeat / animated / playful | More buoyant movement, changing crops, controlled directional shifts, occasional playful offsets | Shorter changes around groove and phrase accents; syncopated timing can work | Clearer contrast, more open highlights, palette chosen from the track—not automatically neon | Random bouncing on every beat, childish graphics unless requested, relentless movement |
| Danceable / club / groove-led | Rhythmic camera accents, repeatable movement motifs, framing changes on bars and fills | Cuts on phrase/bar changes and selected syncopations; transitions should land with the groove | Punchier contrast and defined highlights; maintain low-end visual weight without a generic spectrum | Cutting on every kick, constant shake, full-screen equalizers, unearned flashes |
| Motivational / uplifting / triumphant | Forward movement, gradual widening/reveal, stronger stable compositions as the track rises | Build toward a real lift/drop/arrival; resolve on the strongest phrase | Increasing openness or brightness when music genuinely rises; confident typography if needed | Stock motivational slogans, fake climaxes, arbitrary speed ramps |
| Dark / aggressive / tense | More decisive framing, brief controlled jolts, stronger directional moves | Precise cuts on real impacts, tension holds before release, concise transitions | Deeper contrast, sharper highlights, selective distortion/texture only when sonically justified | Random glitch, overdone chromatic aberration, continuous camera shake |
| Nostalgic / lo-fi / warm | Gentle drift, imperfect but controlled framing, intimate details | Relaxed phrase-led rhythm; small visual imperfections can mark transitions | Fine grain, softened highlights, subtle warmth or faded tone when supported | Default VHS overlays, fake timestamp/UI, excessive dust and scratches |
| Energetic but emotionally dark | Dynamic movement with composed, serious framing; let groove move while emotional image stays restrained | Strong rhythmic accents contrasted with longer emotional holds | Darker emotional palette with clear accents; avoid flattening everything into black | Assuming high energy requires cheerful color or playful typography |

A mood can combine multiple rows. Pick the dominant emotional character, then use groove and energy as separate controls. State which traits lead and which provide contrast.

### Common combinations: resolve the contradiction deliberately

- Melancholic + danceable: sustained, atmospheric composition; selected syncopated camera/scale accents; no constant bounce.
- Sad + high energy: emotionally restrained imagery with urgent pacing or rhythmic impact; do not turn sadness into cheerful color.
- Dreamy + upbeat: soft layered space with buoyant movement and clear accents; do not make it sleepy.
- Motivational + slow build: controlled opening, gradual visual expansion, and a clear payoff at the actual lift.
- Dark + playful: retain darker tonal identity while using syncopated timing or small unexpected framing changes; do not automatically add horror imagery.
- Nostalgic + club: tactile texture and a clear groove; keep rhythmic motion precise instead of adding generic VHS effects.

The contradiction may be the track's strongest identity. Preserve it; do not resolve every mixed emotion into one simple aesthetic.

## 3. Understand style labels such as "vibe" and "edit"

These words are not complete creative briefs.

- "Vibe" describes an atmosphere-led experience, not a fixed tempo, color, or motion level. Ask what feeling and sonic details define this vibe, or infer them from the track and references. It can be warm, bright, eerie, euphoric, melancholic, playful, or spacious.
- "Edit" usually describes a short-form editing treatment or culture/style, not a mood. Determine whether the user means a fast beat edit, fan edit, aesthetic edit, montage, performance edit, or simply a polished Reel. Do not assume all edits require rapid cuts, speed ramps, shakes, or flashes.
- "Animated" can mean the music feels lively, or that the visuals should move. Confirm through context; do not confuse track energy with animation quantity.
- "Danceable" describes groove and body movement, not necessarily happy emotion or high loudness.
- "Motivational" describes emotional direction or intent, not necessarily fast BPM.
- "Melancholic" is not identical to "sad"; it can be reflective, nostalgic, bittersweet, beautiful, or quietly hopeful.

When a user supplies a reference, identify which quality they want to borrow (pace, motion, transition, framing, color, emotional tone, or structure). Do not copy the whole reference style by default.

## 4. Make motion specific and musically justified

For every significant animated event, define:
1. Timecode in the source audio/video.
2. Musical trigger confirmed by listening (kick, snare, bass entrance, chord change, fill, pause, melodic turn, phrase boundary, or reverb tail).
3. Visual action (scale, position, rotation, crop, light, texture, type, or transition).
4. Amplitude and duration appropriate to the track.
5. Easing and recovery so the move settles naturally.
6. Purpose (emphasis, reveal, tension, release, depth, or emotional support).

Use these as starting ranges, adapting them to source framing and mood:
- Micro pulse: about 1–1.5% scale change.
- Clear accent: about 1.5–2.5%.
- Strong phrase impact: up to about 3–4% only when the source can tolerate it and the moment earns it.
- Gentle atmospheric drift: often more effective through position/focus than repeated zooms.
- Rotation should remain subtle for intimate or spacious music; stronger rotation is a deliberate event, not a constant movement layer.

Use different easing for different jobs: smooth ease-in/out for drift, sharper ease-out with a controlled settle for an impact, and restrained spring only when the music genuinely feels playful. Never apply one identical pulse to every beat.

### Beat rules

- Listen and confirm the transient before syncing a visual.
- Prefer selected phrase accents, bar boundaries, fills, bass changes, and meaningful kicks/snare hits.
- Do not pulse on every kick or quantize every transition to the beat.
- If the track is beatless, rubato, or mostly sustained, use melodic phrasing, harmonic changes, texture entrances, and reverb tails instead.
- Do not infer that a visual object caused a sound unless the footage genuinely shows that event.

## 5. Design the hook and attention arc for the track's character

The first frame and first audible moment should make the viewer recognize the experience being offered. A strong hook is not always a fast cut.

- High-energy / dance: open on a distinctive groove, bass/drum entrance, rhythmic motif, or striking real project frame. Give the first accent a clear visual landing.
- Melancholic / sad / intimate: open on the strongest emotional phrase, melody, or evocative real frame. Use focus, framing, or a small reveal instead of a shock transition.
- Dreamy / vibe: establish a distinctive sound and visual space immediately; begin the atmosphere in motion rather than fading up from nothing.
- Motivational / build: establish tension or direction, then let the opening promise a real musical lift. Do not tease a payoff that the excerpt never delivers.
- Playful / upbeat: use a recognizable rhythmic or melodic detail and a confident, buoyant framing change; keep the visual timing clean.

Use a short attention arc appropriate to the clip length:
**hook → orientation → development/contrast → musical payoff → resolution or loop**.

An attention reset can be a crop reveal, changed camera direction, new musical layer, lighting shift, text appearing at a phrase, a held close-up, or a transition. It does not have to be a cut or effect. If the track becomes quieter, the edit may become simpler rather than fighting the music.

## 6. Produce a usable direction sheet

For substantial edits, include this in the edit brief or save it as MUSIC-DIRECTION.md:

| Field | Required note |
|---|---|
| Track / selected excerpt | Exact source and start/end timecodes |
| Energy | 1–5, with one sentence of audible evidence |
| Groove / dance drive | 1–5, with evidence |
| Valence | -2 to +2, with evidence |
| Tension / density / space | Each 1–5, with evidence |
| Primary + secondary mood | Precise words; note any contradiction |
| Section trajectory | Build, drop, release, suspension, plateau, contrast, etc. |
| Signature musical events | Timestamped list |
| Listener feeling | What the right viewer should feel, not a demographic guess |
| Visual grammar | Camera, cut cadence, transitions, light, color, texture, typography |
| Motion intensity | Expected baseline and strongest justified accents |
| Hook and payoff | First-frame/audible promise and where it is paid off |
| Ending | Phrase resolution, natural loop, end card, or deliberate cut |

For a minor change to a well-understood edit, the notes can be brief. For multiple outputs, profile each chosen excerpt and define a distinct editorial job for each video.

## 7. Final creative checks

Before rendering, ask:

- Does the visual energy match the perceived energy, not just the BPM?
- Does the movement respect groove and syncopation?
- Does the palette and texture reflect the emotional character?
- Have mixed moods been preserved rather than flattened?
- Does each significant effect have an audible or narrative reason?
- Is the edit too passive for an energetic track, or too busy for a spacious one?
- Does the hook fit the song, rather than imitate an unrelated viral format?
- Is the payoff genuinely present in the selected excerpt?
- Does the Drazyx identity support the track rather than force every song into one dark-purple preset?

If any answer is no, revise the visual grammar before adding more effects.

## 8. Non-negotiable boundaries

- Do not equate fast BPM with high energy, minor key with sadness, loudness with impact, or syncopation with danceability without listening.
- Do not make every Drazyx edit dark, purple, slow, grainy, or atmospheric by default.
- Do not make every energetic edit fast-cut, shaky, glitch-heavy, or saturated.
- Do not force every sad edit into a slow-motion cliché.
- Do not add a visual pulse when there is no meaningful musical trigger.
- Do not promise replay, shares, retention, or virality. These are creative hypotheses to test against actual analytics.
- Preserve the user's audio, footage, rights, and explicit creative constraints.
